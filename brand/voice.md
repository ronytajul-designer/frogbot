# FrogBot voice guide

Status: working draft by the designer's team. Not yet approved by the client. Example lines below are drafts, not approved copy.

## Who we are talking to

A founder or operator. They pay for several per-seat SaaS tools, they are tired of the bill, and they are not sure a replacement is safe. They are smart, busy, and often not technical. They decide with money and risk in mind, not with framework features.

## Where the voice comes from

The redesign follows the tone used by the leading companies in developer tools and business software. These are patterns observed across those companies' public sites, not quotes.

| Reference | What they do | What FrogBot takes |
|---|---|---|
| Stripe | State the outcome first, then show the proof. Precise numbers, no adjectives doing the work. | Every claim sits next to its evidence (price, code, live demo). |
| Linear | Short, calm, confident sentences. No exclamation marks, no hype words. | Confidence through restraint, not volume. |
| Vercel | One clear promise per headline. Developer terms only where a developer is reading. | One idea per headline. Technical detail lives lower on the page. |
| Supabase | Friendly and plain. Open source and "no lock-in" are explained as practical benefits. | Ownership is described as something you can check: export, self-host, MIT license. |
| 37signals (ONCE, Basecamp) | Argues against the SaaS rental model itself, in plain speech, with a clear point of view. | Attack the per-seat model, not the people who use it. |

## The voice in four rules

1. **Plain before clever.** A founder should understand every headline without knowing what RBAC or MIT means. Add the technical term after the plain one.
2. **Specific before bold.** "$29 per app, unlimited users" beats "radically cheaper". Numbers, time frames, and named tools carry the message.
3. **Confident, not hostile.** Criticise seat pricing and lock-in. Do not mock competitors or their customers.
4. **Honest about limits.** Say what FrogBot does not do, what is included, and what costs extra. For a risky switch, admitted limits build more trust than big promises.

## Do and do not

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| "Pay for the app. Never for the seat." | "Insanely affordable pricing" |
| "Your code and data stay yours. Export or self-host any time." | "Unlock total freedom" |
| "A working prototype in 48 hours." | "Lightning fast delivery" |
| "Replace Airtable with an app you own." | "Airtable is dead." |
| "Built on an MIT-licensed framework (free, open source)." | "Powered by our next-gen stack" |

## Words

- Use: own, replace, per app, unlimited users, export, self-host, prototype, migrate, flat price.
- Avoid: revolutionary, seamless, supercharge, game-changing, 10x, unleash, vibe code (unclear to non-technical buyers).
- Technical terms (RBAC, MCP, collections, npx): allowed in developer sections and docs, always after the plain-language benefit.

## Mechanics

- Sentence case for headlines and buttons.
- Headlines: 8 words or fewer where possible. One idea each.
- Buttons say what happens next: "Deploy first app", "Book a build call".
- No exclamation marks.
- Numbers as digits, with the unit: "$29 per app per month", "48 hours".

## Decision on "Killer" language

Decided by the designer on 2026-11-14: keep "The SaaS Killer" as the brand line, soften everything else.

- "The SaaS Killer." stays as the brand line.
- Solution pages and section copy say "Replace Airtable", not "Kill Airtable".
- No rotating competitor names in the headline ("The Salesforce Killer.", "The Zendesk Killer."). One promise for every visitor.
- Criticism targets seat pricing and lock-in, never a competitor's customers.
